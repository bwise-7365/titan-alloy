Dealing with long / non-terminating MCTS rollouts in Latrunculi — literature review + recommendation                                                 

 Context (the problem)

 The shared MCTS (absgame/mcts.cpp) uses uniform-random rollouts capped at
 kMaxRolloutDepth = 200, falling back to staticEval() (the v(M,N) material-balance score) when
 the cap is hit. For Latrunculi this rollout is nearly worthless: random play almost never sets up a
 custodial capture, so (a) rollouts almost never reach a terminal (hence terminalCount ≈ 0), and
 (b) because random play barely changes material, the v(M,N) returned at the cap ≈ v(M,N) at the
 rollout's start node — the 200 random moves add cost but little information. MCTS is effectively
 doing noisy single-node evaluation. This is a known weakness of vanilla MCTS in tactical,
 slow-to-terminate games (Świechowski et al. review; Baier & Winands). Goal: make MCTS informative
 on Latrunculi without harming the irrgo/mancala GUIs that share mcts.cpp.

 Three literature-backed approaches

 A. Informed ("heavy") playouts — capture-biased ε-greedy, with a decisive-move shortcut

 Replace the uniform rollout policy with a domain-biased one: with probability ε play a uniform
 random move (keep exploration), otherwise play a heuristically "good" — here aggressive
 (capturing / removing / immobilising) — move; and as a cheap special case, if a move wins
 immediately, take it (Teytaud & Teytaud "decisive moves"). Heavy playouts (Drake & Uurtamo 2007)
 and ε-greedy playouts are the standard fix for uninformative random rollouts; ε≈0.3 is a common
 sweet spot and ε=0 (greedy-only) is known to be harmful (Scopone, Amazons studies). This makes
 rollouts actually reduce material → reach reduction/immobilisation terminals more often, and the
 truncated v(M,N) reflects realistic aggressive play rather than aimless shuffling.

 B. Eval-as-leaf — drastic truncation, evaluation replaces the rollout

 Cut the rollout to ~0 plies (or a handful) and back up staticEval() directly. Literature on
 truncated rollouts / early playout termination shows the optimal cutoff is often very early
 (~6 moves), and even a weak evaluation beats a long random playout ("Using evaluation functions
 in MCTS"; review paper). Since Latrunculi's random rollout adds almost nothing, replacing it with a
 direct v(M,N) is cheap and gives many more tree iterations per second.

 C. MCTS-minimax node priors / implicit-minimax backups

 Use a shallow minimax with the v(M,N) eval to seed node priors (and/or keep a minimax-backed value
 beside the visit-average). Baier & Winands (JAIR 2018) found minimax-computed node priors the
 strongest of their hybrids in tactical domains (Othello, Breakthrough, Catch-the-Lion); "implicit
 minimax backups" (Lanctot et al. 2014) is the lighter cousin. Addresses MCTS's trap-blindness, but
 is the heaviest lift and overlaps the project's existing negamax searcher.

 Recommendation: A — capture-biased ε-greedy heavy playout, with the decisive-move shortcut

 Best fit for the Latrunculi project because:
 - It attacks the actual cause — random play never captures. Biasing toward captures/removals makes
 rollouts progress toward decisive endings (so terminalCount rises and rollouts terminate) and
 makes the truncated v(M,N) meaningful. It improves both symptoms at once, where B only papers over
 one and C is overkill.
 - It is exactly the user's instinct, and the best-validated, moderate-cost option in the
 literature (heavy playouts / ε-greedy / decisive moves are decades-proven, low-overhead).
 - It can be added per-game so irrgo and mancala are untouched — important since mcts.cpp is
 shared. B's simplest form is a shared change that risks the other two games.
 - The engine already exposes everything needed to recognise an aggressive move: a movement MoveId
 decodes to (removeSquare, from, to) (a removal is decisive material loss for the opponent), and
 the existing pinnedOn / boundDiscs logic identifies a move that immobilises an enemy.

 If minimal effort is ever preferred over peak strength, B is the cheap fallback (and A's policy
 can also justify lowering kMaxRolloutDepth, since biased rollouts settle faster). They compose:
 A + a shorter cap is a reasonable end state.

 Sketch of the recommended implementation (for a later pass)

 - absgame/AbsGame.h — add a virtual rollout-policy hook, default = uniform random:
 virtual MoveId chooseRolloutMove(const std::vector<MoveId>& legal, std::mt19937_64& rng) const;
 Base implementation picks uniformly (so irrgo/mancala are unchanged).
 - absgame/mcts.cpp — in rollout(), replace the uniform dist(rng) pick with
 game->chooseRolloutMove(moves, rng). Keep the depth cap + staticEval fallback.
 - latrunculi_game/Game.{h,cpp} — override chooseRolloutMove: with prob ε (~0.4) return a
 uniform pick; else prefer, in order, (1) an immediately-winning move (decisive), (2) a
 capture/removal-creating move (a removeSquare >= 0 removal, or a move that raises
 boundDiscs(opp)), (3) uniform random. Keep detection cheap (decode the MoveId; only
 scratch-apply a small sampled subset, never all moves every ply) so rollouts stay fast.
 - Tune ε on self-play; consider lowering kMaxRolloutDepth once rollouts terminate sooner.

 Verification (user builds)

 cmake --build build --target latrunculi_lib latrunculi_selfplay latrunculi_gui. Run a movement-phase
 MCTS: terminalCount should become clearly non-zero, and MCTS move quality (and self-play decisiveness)
 should improve over the uniform-rollout baseline. Confirm irrgo/mancala MCTS behaviour is unchanged
 (they keep the default uniform policy).

---------------------
User notes: shallow cut-offs would probably not work very well in Latrunculi unless the static evaluation included factors other than
material balance. Again, because the first 40 moves or so are prohibited from capture, even a 10-deep lookahead will yield nothing 
for the first 30 placements at least. In the movement phase, it would confront the same problem of random moves being very unlikely
to capture. I suspect a useful static evaluation function would have to include some of the classic factors from Chess, such
as mobility, control of central areas, and so on. (Yes, I know that deep fast search with plain material balance does better
than sophisticated measures over fewer positions. Neural nets beat both.)
