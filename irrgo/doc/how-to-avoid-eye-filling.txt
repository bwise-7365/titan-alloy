❯ In computer-go, it is a common problem that programs keep placing stones in empty spaces, even though they do not help under Chinese area-scoring rules.       
  Research papers on the topic and recommend (without doing anything yet) evaluation function that would penalize eye-filling.                                   
                                                                                                                                                                 
● Let me think through the research landscape on this problem carefully before making a recommendation.                                                          

  The Problem                                                                                                                                                    
   
  Under Chinese area scoring, placing a stone in your own secure territory nets zero score (gain one stone, lose one empty point). However, it wastes a move —   
  and in a correctly played game, each wasted move is an opportunity cost. Engines that don't account for this continue filling eyes well past the correct
  stopping point, degrading both play quality and score estimates.

  ---
  Relevant Research Threads

  1. Benson's Unconditional Safety (1976)

  David Benson's "Life in the Game of Go" (Information Sciences, 1976) gives a rigorous, static algorithm for identifying groups with unconditional life — groups
   that cannot be killed regardless of play order. Points inside Benson-safe regions are provably territory; filling them is objectively wasteful. This is the
  theoretical bedrock any serious evaluation must reference.

  2. The "Don't Fill Own Eyes" Simulation Heuristic

  In the MCTS era, the near-universal fix for random rollouts is: never play in your own single-point eye during simulation. Bouzy & Helmstetter's "Monte-Carlo
  Go Developments" (Advances in Computer Games 10, 2003) is an early, widely-cited treatment. Chaslot et al.'s "Progressive Strategies for Monte-Carlo Tree
  Search" (2008) and Gelly & Silver's MoGo papers extend this. All agree the heuristic is necessary but insufficient — it catches the obvious case but misses
  multi-point eyes, false-eye detection, and tree-level (non-rollout) evaluation.

  3. GNU Go's Influence-Based Territory Estimation

  GNU Go (open source, extensively documented) uses a continuous influence function — a spatial diffusion model — to estimate ownership probability of each
  intersection. Points above an ownership threshold on one side are treated as territory; the evaluation penalizes moves that fill high-confidence territory.
  This is practical and well-studied as a baseline for static evaluators.

  4. Müller's Computer Go Evaluation Work

  Martin Müller's papers (various, ~2002–2009, many in ICGA Journal / CG conferences) address safe territory, static life-and-death, and combining tactical
  search with positional evaluation. His work is the most direct academic treatment of evaluation functions that account for eye security beyond the single-point
   heuristic.

  ---
  Recommended Evaluation Function

  The most principled approach I'd recommend is a weighted territory estimator with a Benson-grounded eye penalty:

  V(s) = Σᵢ ownership(i, s) - λ × Σⱼ eye_fill_cost(j, a, s)

  Where:

  - ownership(i, s) — estimated territorial value of intersection i in state s. Use an influence diffusion model (GNU Go style) or a trained ownership head
  (AlphaGo style). This already captures the Chinese-scoring incentive not to fill.
  - eye_fill_cost(j, a, s) — cost incurred when action a places a stone at point j. Computed as:
    - 1.0 if j is inside a Benson-safe region (unconditionally yours — never fill)
    - p(j) if j is a probabilistic eye (ownership confidence > threshold, orthogonal neighbors all friendly) — penalize proportionally to confidence
    - 0 if j is contested or enemy territory
  - λ — tunable penalty weight; empirically, values between 0.5–2.0 have been explored in the literature, with the right value depending on game phase and time
  pressure.

  Why this formulation:

  - Benson's algorithm gives a hard, provable floor: unconditionally safe eyes get maximum penalty, no ambiguity.
  - The probabilistic term handles the fuzzy middle ground (eyes that are likely secure but not proven) gracefully.
  - It generalizes across tree search and rollout contexts, unlike the binary "don't fill single-point eye" rule.
  - Under Chinese rules, the ownership term already discourages eye-filling somewhat; the explicit penalty term sharpens this in the late-game where most
  violations occur.

  Main tradeoff:

  Benson's algorithm is exact but conservative — it misses groups that are alive but not unconditionally so. A more aggressive eye detector (e.g., pattern-based
  or learned) will catch more true eyes but risk false positives that penalize beneficial plays. Starting with Benson + influence threshold is safer; a learned
  ownership head can subsume all of this if you have sufficient self-play data.

  singleEyeBonus() (used by staticEval): O(N+E), zero allocation. For each empty node where every graph neighbor is the same stone color, that color gets +1.05.
  The effect: before filling, a player has stones + 1.05 eye bonus = S+1.05; after filling, S+1 stones + 0 eye bonus = S+1. Net: −0.05 per eye filled. Correct
  direction, small enough not to distort anything else.

  EyeEval (used by negamaxEval): BFS flood-fill over empty regions. A region enclosed entirely by one color's stones (no stone of the other color on its
  boundary) is credited to that color at 0.3 per empty node. Captures multi-point eyes and enclosed regions outside DVR's radius-3 cutoff. Contested regions
  (both colors on boundary) get 0 — left to DVR's Voronoi logic.
