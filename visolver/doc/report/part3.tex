\chapter{Testing and Lessons Learned}
\label{ch:testing}

This chapter is the library's evidence. It describes how the test suite is
built and what each layer of it checks (Sections~\ref{sec:t-infra}--%
\ref{sec:t-protocol}); then the application problems as case studies ---
the banded flow-planning QP and its fleet generalization, the influence
game, the deployment game, and the GMS front end that mechanizes problem
entry; the flow and deployment studies carry equal
weight as the paired monotone-at-scale and nonmonotone stories
(Sections~\ref{sec:t-flow}--\ref{sec:t-gms}); and
closes with the cross-cutting lessons and the known open items
(Sections~\ref{sec:t-lessons}--\ref{sec:t-open}). Wherever a number
appears, its source is named: a section of
\code{network/doc/performance.md}, a dated run log, or a test that
reproduces it.

\section{Test philosophy and infrastructure}
\label{sec:t-infra}

The suite is GoogleTest, fetched by CMake \code{FetchContent} at a fixed
release tag and compiled by the project's own toolchain and flags. At the
time of writing (2026-07-09) it stands at 214 test cases, all passing,
exercised by the aggregate targets of Chapter~\ref{ch:manual}. Beneath GoogleTest sits a
thin shared harness (\code{utils.hpp}): a bound solver is a
\code{SolveFn} (everything captured but the start point), a pass/fail
criterion is a \code{CheckFn} returning a one-line report, and
\code{runCase} runs one solve --- timing it, printing the standard
statistics, reporting a throw as that case's failure rather than aborting
the program --- so every solver test in the library is the same procedure
with different bindings. A test-only bridge (\code{testsupport.hpp}) maps
the harness onto GoogleTest assertions without ever letting GoogleTest leak
into the shipped library.

The suite divides into three layers with different jobs:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unit tests} verify the building blocks: line searches,
  Levenberg--Marquardt primitives, the FD Jacobian, projectors, the config
  parser, instance generators.
\item \textbf{Protocol tests} verify the \emph{control logic} of engines
  --- rescue paths, stall cutoffs, chain rounds --- by injecting
  deliberately failing or fake components (Section~\ref{sec:t-protocol}).
\item \textbf{Acceptance tests} verify end-to-end behavior on the three
  application problems, checked against author-verified answers
  (Sections~\ref{sec:t-flow}--\ref{sec:t-deploy}).
\end{itemize}

The checks are written to remain correct under multiplicity. Where a
problem has one verified answer, the test requires both convergence and a
match. Where legitimate outcomes vary --- nonmonotone games where
individual engines may legitimately fail, or multiple equilibria --- the
check is expressed over a set: ``at least $k$ of these rows pass'' (with
$k$ set to the known-good count, so a regression in any known-good row is
caught), or ``the answer matches \emph{some} entry of a verified roster.''
A converged answer that matches no roster entry prints itself in
paste-ready roster form, so charting a newly discovered equilibrium is one
verification away from a one-line addition.

\section{The minimal problems}
\label{sec:t-minimal}

A collection of small constructed-solution problems exercises every engine
quickly and exactly. Each family checks something specific:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Constructed LCPs} (\code{makeComplementaryPair}: draw a
  complementary $(w^\ast, z^\ast)$, then set $q = w^\ast - Mz^\ast$). With
  $M = A^\top A$ (PSD by construction) they check correctness of the
  monotone engines (\code{lcp\_psd\_test}); with random indefinite $M$
  (\code{lcp\_random\_test}) they check the \emph{divergence guard itself}
  --- the test asserts the throw. An engine that silently ``converged'' on
  the indefinite instance would be the bug.
\item \textbf{Cubic VIs} (\code{makeCubicProblem}: $F = A^\top g(Ax) + k$
  with elementwise cubic $g$, PSD-forced Jacobian, known solution) drive
  the Josephy--Newton loop end to end; the four-engine comparison test
  (\code{han\_vs\_he\_test}) runs an identical LCP and an identical cubic
  VI through every engine row and requires each to recover the known
  solution --- the library's standing cross-engine consistency check.
\item \textbf{Ellipsoid-$K$ problems} verify the claim that the projection
  engines and the driver are generic in $K$: the same VIs solved over
  \code{makeEllipsoidProjector}'s set, plus the projector's own
  root-finding unit tests.
\item \textbf{Degenerate and rank-deficient LCPs} confirm the property for
  which the interior-point engine was added: strict complementarity broken
  at every third index, $M = A^\top A$ rank-deficient. Iteration counts
  9 / 19 / 10 on the clean / degenerate / rank-deficient instances
  (\code{mehrotra\_ipm\_test}) --- tens, not thousands.
\item \textbf{Mixed hand QPs} (small KKT systems with hand-checkable
  solutions) verify the mixed free/orthant plumbing. One of them exposed a
  defect during development: a mixed QP whose solution sits exactly on a
  Fischer--Burmeister kink revealed a $y$-space/$z$-space confusion in the
  semismooth kink indicator that no pure-LCP test could detect (with
  $n = 0$ the two spaces coincide). The corrected solver then solved that
  QP in 2 iterations with residual exactly zero --- the expected signature
  of Newton on a problem that is affine on the active piece
  (\code{semismooth\_newton\_test}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Protocol testing: fakes, not hard problems}
\label{sec:t-protocol}

The suite's most transferable lesson is methodological. Engine
\emph{protocols} --- rescue paths, drift guards, stall cutoffs, chain
control flow --- are triggered by rare numerical events, and searching for
a natural problem that reliably triggers them is both fragile and, in one
documented case, impossible:

\begin{quote}
The IPM's singularity rescue was long ``covered'' by a test named after
rescuing a singular free block. Probing showed the rescue had \emph{never
fired}: with an exactly singular block, partial-pivot LU leaves a zero
pivot, but the corresponding right-hand-side entry is \emph{structurally}
zero on any consistent system, and $0/0$ evaluated through the
back-substitution yields finite results --- so the non-finite trigger never
trips. Only an \emph{inconsistent} singular system (which the surrounding
algorithm does not produce) throws NaNs. The test was renamed to assert
what it actually shows, and the rescue is now exercised directly.
\end{quote}

The direct method is \emph{dependency injection of failure}: every protocol
test drives its engine with a deliberately broken or fake component bound
through the same extension points production code uses. The
failing-factory test hands \code{mehrotraIpm} a factory whose first
factorization returns NaN and then delegates --- the rescue's
regularization sequence is asserted deterministically. The drift-guard test
hands it an \emph{echo} factory and asserts \code{newtonCheckTol} catches
the inconsistency by name. The Josephy--Newton stall cutoff is proven with
a \emph{frozen} inner solver (returns its start; the outer step is exactly
zero, so the residual provably cannot improve). The alternating chain's
control logic --- best-point memory, throw-as-stall, the improvement
cutoff, perturb-restart --- is verified with fixed-point, throwing, and
identity stages (\code{alternating\_chain\_test}), including the guarantee
that a stagnant chain with perturbation disabled stops after one wasted
round while the same chain with perturbation enabled spends its full round
budget trying. Fakes make the rare path the \emph{common} path,
deterministically, in milliseconds.

\section{Case study 1: the banded flow-planning QP}
\label{sec:t-flow}

The flow-planning pipeline (Chapter~\ref{ch:manual}; mathematics in
Chapter~\ref{ch:math}) was developed against two instance geometries:
\emph{laydown 0} (random square, near-metric costs --- direct arcs
dominate, screens work) and \emph{laydown 1} (banded rectangles,
near-tied costs --- multi-hop routes, wide flat optimal faces, degenerate
by construction). The second is the stress case, and its 200-node instance
drove three generations of solver work. All numbers below are from
\code{network/doc/performance.md} (sections P1--P9) and the dated run
logs of 2026-07-05/06; the keep-all configuration is reproducible from
\code{network/ns3-keepall.cfg} (seed base 20260704).

\paragraph{Generation 1: projection-contraction + screens.} On spread
geometry the screened pipeline performs well: the 70-node profile solves in
0.15--0.9\,s with the screen versus 144\,s keep-all ($\sim$160$\times$),
and the certificate loop confirms the screened answer identical to 5--6
digits (P1--P3). On the 200-node \emph{banded} instance the same engine
hits its 150{,}000-iteration cap ($\sim$6 minutes) with no answer at LCP
dimension $\sim$1{,}900 --- the flat-face stall of Chapter~\ref{ch:math},
here on a full-scale instance. Tightened tolerances, certificate slack,
and tie-break adjustments all failed to rescue it (P4--P5): the problem
was the engine class, not the tuning.

\paragraph{Generation 2: the interior-point engine.} The same instance
under \code{engine = ipm} (dense LU), screened, one certificate round:
convergence in 35 iterations at Newton dimension 10{,}838 (the size of the
linear system factored each iteration) --- the degeneracy-insensitivity
holding at scale --- but 29.6 minutes of wall time, nearly all of it dense
$O(d^3)$ factorization ($\sim$50\,s per iteration), and a correctly
reported \code{certifiedP = false} after a single certificate round grew
the kept set from $\sim$1{,}900 to 10{,}737 of 14{,}500 pairs (run log
2026-07-06; P6--P7). The verdict: the iteration-count problem was solved,
and the cost had moved into the linear algebra and the screening
formulation.

\paragraph{Generation 3: structured linear algebra, no screen.} With the
flow Newton factory (Chapter~\ref{ch:math}) and no screen at all --- all
14{,}500 pairs kept, hence nothing to certify --- the same instance solves
in 36 iterations and 4.51 seconds, certified exact by construction, at
Newton dimension 14{,}601 (P8--P9). That is $\sim$394$\times$ faster than
the screened dense-LU path and strictly better: the screened one-round
answer had overshot the optimum by $\sim$0.4\% ($\theta = 53.117$ vs.\
$52.908$). The banded/large production default is now \code{ipm} +
\code{ipmNewton = flow} + no screen; small spread-geometry instances keep
screened \code{bsHe94b}.

Three findings generalize beyond this problem. First, iteration count and
per-iteration cost are independent axes: generation 2 fixed the first and
was still unusable until generation 3 fixed the second. Second, screens
trade optimality silently: the 0.4\% overshoot came from a correctly
implemented, certificate-checked pipeline whose round budget ran out;
exactness by construction outperformed exactness by certification in both
time and quality. Third, nondimensionalization is mandatory: the exact
rescaling of Chapter~\ref{ch:math} took the small lane-solve benchmark
from 25\,s to 0.04\,s before any algorithmic work at all (P1).

\section{Visual validation: the flow-plan and fleet viewers}
\label{sec:t-viewers}

The numeric suites assert exact values and tolerances; some checks resist
assertion. Whether a plan follows the geometry, whether a sparsified plan
visibly consolidates, and whether a rationing decision distributes shortage
plausibly are judgments a person makes in seconds from a picture and only
laboriously from a matrix dump. Two optional Qt~6 viewers serve these
checks: \code{network\_viewer} for the single-commodity flow-planning
problem of Section~\ref{sec:t-flow}, and \code{fleet\_viewer} for its
multi-vehicle, multi-asset generalization
(\code{network/doc/fleet-formulation.md}). Both are excluded from the
solver and test build when Qt is absent. This section describes the
viewers and states, in pseudo-code, the three planning algorithms behind
their buttons, at the depth needed to re-implement them --- e.g.\ in
Java~17. Conventions for the pseudo-code: every quantity is a
\code{double} (nothing in these models is integer, including vehicle
counts); indices are 0-based; \code{argmax}/\code{argmin} break ties by
lowest index, which makes runs reproducible; and dense
$\mathit{numNodes} \times \mathit{numNodes}$ arrays are adequate at these
sizes ($\le$ a few hundred nodes), so no linear-algebra library is
required beyond row and column sums.

\subsection{Pseudo-code: the greedy planner}
\label{sec:t-greedy-pseudo}

The greedy planner (\code{network/lib/greedy.cpp}) runs in two phases.
Phase 1 answers ``who gets shorted when demand exceeds capacity'' with no
reference to transport at all. It minimizes the priority-weighted
quadratic shortfall $\sum_i P_i \left((D_i - R_i)/D_i\right)^2$ subject
to $\sum_i R_i \le M$, where $M = \min(\sum_i D_i, \sum_i C_i)$ is the
meetable total. This is a very simple instance of the \emph{continuous
quadratic knapsack problem}---the quadratic case of the continuous, separable,
convex resource-allocation problem~\cite{patriksson2008,patrikssonStromberg2015},
itself just a special case of the monotone complementarity problems solved
elsewhere in this report. Its optimum has the closed form
$R_i = D_i - \lambda D_i^2 / P_i$ with one scalar multiplier $\lambda$;
entries driven negative are clamped to zero, removed from the active set,
and $\lambda$ is re-solved over the survivors---the standard finite
\emph{pegging} (variable-fixing) algorithm~\cite{frangioniGorgone2013}. (It is
\emph{not} the information-theory water-filling problem, whose different
objective yields the additive $p_i=(\mu-n_i)_+$ form; only the
raise-a-multiplier-until-the-budget-binds skeleton is shared.)

\begin{lstlisting}
scqkp(D, P, M):                          // returns targets R
  if sum(D) <= M: return D               // everything is meetable
  active = { i : D[i] > 0 }
  loop:                                  // at most |active| rounds
    lambda = (sum of D[i] - M) / (sum of D[i]^2 / P[i])   // both over active
    if lambda <= 0: R[i] = D[i] on active; return R
    if D[i] - lambda * D[i]^2 / P[i] >= 0 for all active i:
      R[i] = that value on active; return R
    remove every i with a negative value from active      // their R[i] = 0
\end{lstlisting}

Phase 2 builds direct-arc flows toward the rationed targets. Targets are
first scaled by $1 - 10^{-4}$ so total remaining demand is strictly below
total capacity and a source with remaining capacity always exists. Each
pass serves the largest remaining target from its cheapest source. One
floating-point detail is required for termination and must survive
translation: whichever bound the shipped quantity hits is \emph{assigned}
zero rather than decremented, because \code{x -= q} can leave a
$10^{-18}$ sliver that the loop then shaves forever. With that discipline each pass zeroes
one target or one capacity, so the loop ends within
$\#\text{sinks} + \#\text{sources}$ passes.

\begin{lstlisting}
greedyPlan(C, D, P, cost):
  target = scqkp(D, P, min(sum(D), sum(C)))
  remT = (1 - 1e-4) * target;  remC = C;  flow = 0
  loop:
    n = argmax_i remT[i];  if remT[n] <= 0: break
    s = argmin over { i : remC[i] > 0 } of cost[i][n]
    q = min(remT[n], remC[s])
    flow[s][n] += q
    remT[n] = (q == remT[n]) ? 0 : remT[n] - q   // assign the bound exactly
    remC[s] = (q == remC[s]) ? 0 : remC[s] - q
  supplied[i] = row sum of flow;  resupply[j] = column sum of flow
  suggestedBudget = 0.8 * sum over (i, j) of cost[i][j] * flow[i][j]
\end{lstlisting}

The final line is the planner's second product: the ton-mile budget $L$
handed to the optimizer is calibrated to 80\% of the greedy plan's own
usage, which forces the optimizer to route better than the baseline
rather than merely replicate it.

\subsection{Pseudo-code: swap to optimum}
\label{sec:t-swap-pseudo}

A \emph{swap} is the classical transportation 2-exchange: move $x$ tons
from arcs $(i,j)$ and $(m,n)$ onto the crossed arcs $(i,n)$ and $(m,j)$.
Every node's total inflow and outflow are unchanged, so $S$, $R$, and the
shortfall objective are invariant; only the routing cost moves, by
$x \cdot \left[(c_{ij} + c_{mn}) - (c_{in} + c_{mj})\right]$.

\begin{lstlisting}
bestSwap(cost, flow):
  arcs = all off-diagonal (i, j) with flow[i][j] > 1e-9
  best = none
  for each unordered pair (i,j), (m,n) from arcs
      with i != m, j != n, i != n, m != j:          // degenerate pairs skipped
    perUnit = (cost[i][j] + cost[m][n]) - (cost[i][n] + cost[m][j])
    saving  = min(flow[i][j], flow[m][n]) * perUnit
    keep the pair with the largest saving
  return best

swapToOptimum(cost, flow):
  while bestSwap's saving > 1e-6:                   // absolute ton-miles
    apply it: flow[i][j] -= x; flow[m][n] -= x;
              flow[i][n] += x; flow[m][j] += x      // x = min of the two
\end{lstlisting}

Termination follows from the $10^{-6}$ ton-mile acceptance floor: total
cost decreases by more than that each pass and is bounded below. The
amount $x$ equals one of the two reduced flows, so each swap also zeroes
at least one arc, though it may put flow on up to two arcs that were
previously empty --- a swap pass can therefore \emph{raise} the
positive-arc count by one per swap even as cost falls.

\subsection{The optimal plan, and why it arrives full of tiny flows}
\label{sec:t-purify}

The \emph{Optimal Plan} overlay is produced by the generation-3 pipeline
of Section~\ref{sec:t-flow}: the instance is reduced to source--sink
shipments along shortest paths, the resulting convex QP's KKT conditions
are assembled as a monotone mixed linear complementarity problem, and
that system is solved by the Mehrotra predictor--corrector interior-point
engine with the structured per-sink Newton factorization
(\code{engine = ipm}, \code{ipmNewton = flow}, no screen), the ton-mile
budget having been calibrated by a greedy pre-pass as above. The solve
runs on a worker thread and its result is cached per instance.


The first sight of a verified optimal plan was a lattice of hairline
arrows: tonnage split across dozens of near-parallel routes, most
carrying trivial amounts. That spread is a property of the algorithm
class, not an error. The objective is strictly convex in the deliveries
$R$ alone, so the optimal $R^\ast$ is unique while the \emph{flows}
achieving it form a polytope --- every point of it exactly optimal. An
interior-point method follows the central path, which terminates at the
\emph{analytic center} of that optimal face, i.e.\ the point maximally
far from all of its bounding inequalities --- and therefore the routing
that spreads tonnage over every optimal alternative at once. An
edge-following method such as the simplex family terminates instead at a
vertex of the same face, and vertices of network polytopes are
forest-sparse: at most $\#\text{sources} + \#\text{sinks} - 1$ positive
arcs, plus one for a tight budget row.


The difference between interior and corner optima can be illustrated with a very
simple example. Suppose we want to maximize the sum of $x_i$, subject to the
constraint that all $ x_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_i  x_i \le 1.0$.  A solver such as
Simplex will follow edges and reach on of the $x_i = 1$ corners, with all 
the other values at zero. However, an interior method is
likely to arrive at a solution in the middle of the space of optimal solutions
 more like all $x_i = 1/N$.

The \emph{Purify (sparsify)} button converts the former into
(approximately) the latter without a re-solve, by pivoting within the
optimal face: deliveries are never touched, so $\theta^\ast$ is preserved
bit for bit. The pass has two ingredients, both built from the swap of
Section~\ref{sec:t-swap-pseudo}: first, ordinary cost-improving swaps to
a local optimum; then, repeatedly, the best-saving swap that strictly
\emph{reduces} the positive-arc count, accepted even at negative saving
as long as total ton-miles stay within the budget (the objective does not
depend on ton-miles, so budget slack may be spent on sparsity). After
consolidation begins, improving swaps are restricted to those that do not
raise the arc count; an unrestricted improving swap can re-spread exactly
what a spending consolidation removed, and the pair would cycle
indefinitely. With the restriction the arc count is monotone and the pass
terminates. The result is a local optimum under length-4 pivots ---
forest-sparse in practice, though not a certified polytope vertex.

\subsection{The fleet viewer}

The fleet viewer drives the multi-vehicle, multi-asset generalization of
the model. An \emph{asset class} $a$ is a kind of cargo, characterized by
the weight $w_a$ (tons) and cargo area $s_a$ (square feet) of one unit;
supply capacity, demand, and priority become per-(node, asset) matrices
$C_{ia}, D_{ia}, P_{ia}$ in units of that asset. A \emph{vehicle class}
$k$ is a kind of transport, characterized by a weight capacity $T_k$, an
area capacity $A_k$, a fleet size $N_k$ (fractional: availability over
the horizon, not a count of hulls), and a speed $v_k$; over a planning
horizon of $H$ hours the class can run
$B_k = N_k \, v_k \, H$ vehicle-miles, and that budget is \emph{data},
in contrast to the single-commodity model's calibrated limit. Cargo on a
link must fit the vehicles allocated to that link by weight and by area
simultaneously; vehicles of each class circulate (what arrives at a node
departs it), and empty repositioning legs consume budget like loaded
ones.

\subsection{Pseudo-code: the budget-limited fleet greedy}
\label{sec:t-fleetgreedy-pseudo}

Rationing decomposes exactly by asset, because assets share transport but
never supply: phase 1 is \code{scqkp} of
Section~\ref{sec:t-greedy-pseudo} applied to each asset column with its
own meetable total. Phase 2 generalizes the greedy loop with three new
ingredients. First, service order matters here, because the budgets can
run dry mid-plan, so the loop serves the cell with the largest
priority-weighted \emph{fractional} shortfall --- the dimensionless score
makes assets measured in different units comparable. Second, sources are
ranked by round-trip distance, since every loaded leg pays an empty
return leg (vehicles are added to both directions, which satisfies
circulation exactly). Third, cargo is loaded conservatively: one vehicle
of class $k$ carries at most
$\kappa_{ak} = \min(T_k / w_a,\; A_k / s_a)$ units of asset $a$ (each
ratio present only when its denominator is positive; classes with
$\kappa = 0$ cannot carry the asset), which satisfies the weight and the
area constraint at once. Mixing complementary assets in one vehicle could
do better; the greedy forgoes that, and the optimizer planned as future
work will not.

\begin{lstlisting}
fleetGreedy(C, D, P, dist, assets, vehicles, H):
  B[k] = N[k] * v[k] * H                          // vehicle-mile budgets
  for each asset a:
    target[.][a] = scqkp(D[.][a], P[.][a], min of the column sums)
  remT = (1 - 1e-4) * target;  remC = C;  remB = B
  kappa[a][k] as above;  order[a] = classes sorted by kappa[a][k] descending
  loop:                                           // <= 3mA + K + 2 passes
    (n, a) = argmax of P[n][a] * (remT[n][a] / D[n][a])^2
             over cells with remT > 0 not yet marked unservable
    if none: break
    s  = argmin over { i : remC[i][a] > 0 } of rt(i, n)
         where rt(i, n) = dist[i][n] + dist[n][i], or dist[i][i] if i == n
    total = sum over { k : remB[k] > 0, kappa[a][k] > 0 }
            of (remB[k] / rt(s, n)) * kappa[a][k]  // units transportable
    if total <= 0: mark (n, a) unservable; continue // budgets exhausted
    q = min(remT[n][a], remC[s][a], total)
    rest = q
    for k in order[a] with remB[k] > 0 and kappa[a][k] > 0:
      avail = (remB[k] / rt) * kappa[a][k]
      units = min(rest, avail)
      veh   = units / kappa[a][k]
      u[k][s][n] += veh;  if s != n: u[k][n][s] += veh   // empty return leg
      remB[k] = (units == avail) ? 0 : remB[k] - veh * rt // assign exactly
      rest -= units;  if rest <= 0: break
    flow[a][s][n] += q - rest
    decrement remT[n][a] and remC[s][a] by (q - rest), assigning the
      bound exactly when it is hit, as in greedyPlan
\end{lstlisting}

The exact-zero discipline extends to the budgets and is again what bounds
the pass count: every pass zeroes a target cell, zeroes a capacity cell,
drains the last capable budget, or marks a cell unservable, giving the
$3mA + K + 2$ cap for $m$ nodes, $A$ assets, and $K$ vehicle classes.
Alongside the plan the algorithm reports per-class utilization
$\mathit{milesUsed}_k / B_k$ and a \emph{fleet scale hint}: the same loop
re-run with unlimited budgets yields the mileage each class would want,
and the maximum ratio of that mileage to $B_k$ states directly how much
larger the fleet must be for this heuristic to serve every rationed
target. On the default 70-node instance the hint reproduced the observed
delivery shortfall to four digits, which is the property that makes it
trustworthy as a capability-scoping number.

\section{Case study 2: the fleet optimizer at the viewer default}
\label{sec:t-fleet}

The fleet generalization of Section~\ref{sec:t-viewers} replayed the banded
QP's performance history at a larger scale, and its resolution compressed
the three generations of Section~\ref{sec:t-flow} into one instrumented
day (2026-07-08). The episode is recorded as a case study in
\emph{diagnosis}: a bounded probe with falsifiable predictions attributed
the cost before any fix was built, and each fix stage was accepted against
a prediction made in advance. All numbers below are from
\code{network/doc/performance.md} (sections P10--P14) and the dated run
logs of 2026-07-08; the staged plan of record is
\code{2026-07-08-fleet-performance-plan.md} at the repository root, and
every run is reproducible from \code{fleet\_benchmark} (seed 20260704).

\paragraph{The scenario.} The fleet viewer's default problem --- 70 nodes,
4 asset types $\times$ 3 vehicle types, banded laydown, limited fleet ---
ran for about an hour in a Release build without finishing, at which point
the author stopped it. The intended production problems are roughly
200--250 nodes with 10--15 vehicle and 10--15 asset types, so the required
improvement was orders of magnitude, beyond what parameter tuning could
supply. The pipeline in effect was the interior-point engine with its
generic dense-LU factorization, a per-asset screen of six sources per
sink, and up to ten certificate re-solve rounds, over a dense $M$.

\paragraph{The probe.} \code{solveFleetPlan} gained observability hooks
(a per-round report fired \emph{before} each solve, so an aborted run
still states the dimension it was attempting, plus flushed per-iteration
heartbeats), and a probe executable reproduced the viewer's exact
instance under a one-round certificate cap. Four predictions were stated
in advance and all four held. First, iteration counts stayed in the
interior-point band and flat across dimension: 24 iterations at dimension
2{,}900, then 32 at 11{,}849. Second, per-iteration cost followed the
dense $O(d^3)$ law: 0.95\,s per iteration at dimension 2{,}900 against
$\sim$64\,s at 11{,}849, matching both the cubic ratio and the
single-commodity datum of Section~\ref{sec:t-flow} to a few percent.
Third, one certificate round on banded geometry grew the kept set
4.2$\times$, from 2{,}772 to 11{,}721 of 14{,}451 variables (81\% of
keep-all): a kept pair enters with every capable vehicle type, so the
type catalog multiplies the single-commodity installment effect of P4.
Fourth, the bounded two-round run cost 2{,}071\,s and was correctly
reported uncertified --- rounds~0--1 alone account for 34.5 minutes, and
a third round near keep-all dimension accounts for the observed hour.
The diagnosis: iteration count was never the problem; the cost was dense
cubic linear algebra multiplied by certificate re-solves at a growing
dimension.

\paragraph{The fix.} The same two levers as generation 3 of
Section~\ref{sec:t-flow}, applied in three verified stages. (i) The fleet
Newton system has a rank-one diagonal block per demand cell and thin skew
borders --- one supply column per (source, asset) cell and one budget
column per vehicle type --- so the per-cell Sherman--Morrison inverse and
a symmetric positive-definite dual Schur complement of size
$\mathit{numSupplyCells} + \mathit{numTypes}$ (131 here) replace the
dense factorization. The algebra was verified in Maxima \emph{before}
implementation (\code{network/doc/fleet-newton-check.mac}, nine checks;
formulae in Chapter~\ref{ch:math}). (ii) The factory was implemented
against parity bars (backward error $\le 10^{-12}$ at any conditioning;
direct step parity on well-scaled systems; an end-to-end run under the
engine's own \code{newtonCheckTol} drift guard), and keep-all became the
default: nothing screened out, nothing to certify, exact by construction.
(iii) The engine touches $M$ in exactly three computations --- the field
$Mz + q$, the drift guard, and the built-in dense factory --- so a
matrix-free overload taking an apply-$M$ operator removed the dense $M$
entirely; the dense path is proven unchanged bit for bit by an exact
equality test, and the fleet pipeline supplies an $O(\mathit{numVars})$
structural apply, never assembling $M$ at all.

\section{Case study 3: strategic allocation of effort}
\label{sec:t-saoe}

The influence game (Chapter~\ref{ch:math}) enters the suite twice, and the
contrast is itself a result. The library's original reduced formulation
(\code{saoe.hpp}: effort space only, intermediates eliminated) is solved by
the projection engines --- \code{bsHe94b} reproduces the reference
equilibrium. The \emph{literal} GAMS MCP transcription
(\code{gms\_alloceff\_test}: dimension 87, with the intermediates and
shadow prices as explicit variables) is measurably harder on the same
engines: both projection rows trip their divergence guards within
milliseconds, as the explicit coupling rows make the Josephy--Newton
linearizations indefinite. The two Newton-class engines are unaffected
(run log 2026-07-06):

\begin{itemize}
\item \code{semismoothNewtonSolve}, cold start, FD Jacobian:
  14 iterations, 182\,ms, squared residual $8.2\times 10^{-9}$;
\item Josephy--Newton over the IPM inner: 2 outer / 16 inner
  iterations, 83\,ms, squared residual $1.1\times 10^{-11}$.
\end{itemize}

Both land on the \emph{same} equilibrium, which the author confirmed is
the one PATH reaches on the GAMS side --- and which matches the reduced
implementation's reference equilibrium. Two independent engine classes and
two independent formulations agreeing on an author-verified point is the
strongest transcription-fidelity evidence the suite has; the test is fixed
to that point, and requires \emph{two} passing rows so that neither
known-good engine can mask a regression in the other.

The problem also supplied the library's longest-standing open item, and
its resolution. On the \emph{reduced} formulation, the Josephy--Newton
engines reliably converge to a negative-utility stationary point rather
than the reference equilibrium $E$, from every start tried over the
project's history. The alternating chain of Section~\ref{sec:t-deploy},
pointed at the same formulation from the same deterministic default start,
converges to $E$ (\code{saoe\_chain\_test}, run 2026-07-06, feasibility
and RMSE at \code{saoe\_test}'s own bars) --- its third distinct success
on a nonmonotone game. The forward-backward splitting engine
(\code{fbs\_hyz04\_test}, same day) then added a fourth independent
method-class confirmation of $E$, and showed $E$ robust to a tenfold
change in the model's strength floor $\epsilon$, converging to the same
point at both $\epsilon = 0.0108$ and the historical $\epsilon = 0.1$. A
seeded $20 \times 12$ companion instance (sign-quota-constrained random
rewards; no known equilibrium) converges to a feasible allocation in the
same test, as the scale datum.

\section{Case study 4: the deployment game}
\label{sec:t-deploy}

The deployment game (Chapter~\ref{ch:math}: dimension 450, nonmonotone by
design, poles just outside the orthant, multiple equilibria) is the
library's nonmonotone acceptance challenge, and it was not solved by any
engine as shipped. It was solved by a \emph{composition} built in eight
short diagnostic runs over one day (2026-07-06). The sequence is recorded
in full because each run's failure determined the next run's design, and
because the diagnostic tooling mattered as much as the algorithm.

\paragraph{Runs 1--2: no standalone engine, and why.} On the GAMS interior
start, every engine failed differently. The semismooth solver cut the
squared residual $1.4\times 10^{5} \to 2.1\times 10^{4}$ in 20 iterations
and then stopped on a failed line search \emph{while still descending};
it had not reached a plateau. \code{bsHe94b} threw its divergence guard
immediately (expected on a nonmonotone problem). The Josephy--Newton/IPM
row reduced the squared residual from $1.4\times 10^{5}$ to $22.9$ in one
linearization, then remained at $19.6$ for $\sim$22 outer iterations at
$\sim$1\,s each until its inner IPM threw on a collapsed step length: the
linearization had gone indefinite. Run 2 added the SEMI-style semismooth
variants: nonmonotone memory 4 escaped the iteration-20 failure (85
iterations) but plateaued at $1.2\times 10^{4}$; the $\lambda = 0.95$
restart \emph{visited} $8.2\times 10^{3}$, then ascended and stopped at
$1.7\times 10^{4}$ (the lesson behind returning the best visited iterate);
plain FB failed soonest, in 10 iterations --- Chen--Chen--Kanzow's
critique \cite{chen2000} in miniature. All cold semismooth endpoints
differ greatly from one another at natural-residual norms near 100: the
merit landscape contains many attracting stationary points, and
line-search tuning changes only which one is reached. Meanwhile the IPM's
stall point was \emph{feasible}, with sensible mixed strategies, three
orders of magnitude closer than any cold semismooth run. Run 2 also
motivated two pieces of permanent infrastructure: the Josephy--Newton
no-progress cutoff, and flushed per-iteration heartbeats (the first run of
this problem was killed after minutes of \emph{buffered} silence, losing
all its output).

\paragraph{Run 3: the failure modes are complementary.} A two-phase chain
--- Josephy--Newton/IPM to its stall, semismooth warm-started from the
stall point --- descended $19.6 \to 0.68$ in 52 iterations, four orders of
magnitude below any cold semismooth run, then plateaued. The new
residual-breakdown diagnostic (print the worst natural-residual rows
\emph{by GAMS name}, with their $y$ and $G$ values) localized the plateau
exactly: negative escort allocations ($-0.34$ against $\epsilon = 0.01$)
--- the iterate had settled beside the poles, where the line search fails
against infinite merit values. The obstruction was geometric, not merit
stationarity, and the remedy followed directly: project back onto $K$
between phases.

\paragraph{Runs 4--5: the alternating chain, hardened, solves it.}
Alternating rounds of project $\to$ globalize $\to$ finish exposed two
robustness requirements in one run: round 2's re-linearized IPM was
\emph{improving} ($1.19 \to 1.07$) when its inner solver threw, and the
escaping exception destroyed a $0.68$ best point --- hence
\emph{throw-as-stall} with best-point memory (and, later, the IPM's
step-length collapse became a non-converged return in the library
itself). With those in place, run 5 converged in round 4 to squared
residual $6.0\times 10^{-15}$ in 17.7\,s --- with the semismooth quadratic
tail visible in the log's last three iterations
($3.8\times 10^{-5} \to 7.1\times 10^{-8} \to 6.0\times 10^{-15}$) --- and
the author confirmed the answer matches the GAMS solution.

\paragraph{Runs 6--8: determinism, perturbation, and a second
equilibrium.} Promoting the chain into the library alongside two solver
semantics changes legitimately shifted the trajectory; the new path
reached $0.145$ \emph{in the correct basin} and stagnated --- and runs 6
and 7 produced digit-for-digit identical output, a direct demonstration
that a deterministic round map at a fixed point cannot be rescued by any
stopping-rule adjustment. The remedy, following PATH's practice
\cite{ferris1998}, is scaled perturbation on stagnation: perturb the
restart by $U[-1,1]\cdot s\cdot\sqrt{\rho(\mathrm{best})}$, vanishing as
the chain approaches a solution, drawn from a fixed seed so runs stay
reproducible. Run 8 converged in round 4 ($1.2\times 10^{-9}$, 28.8\,s)
--- to a \emph{different, equally valid} Nash equilibrium: same strategy
supports, mirror-image probability-cap pattern (Red's cap active where
the GAMS point has Blue's). The acceptance test became a
verified-equilibrium roster with both entries, and the perturbation
acquired a second function: sampling the equilibrium set, which is the
author's stated method for charting it.

\subsection{The cost of interpreted evaluation}
\label{sec:t-gms-cost}

A parsed model evaluates its $F$ by walking expression trees, and on the
two largest models that cost --- not any defect of the mathematics ---
decided the outcome. Three factors multiply.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Interpretation.} Each evaluation walks \code{Expr} trees
  with name-keyed binding lookups and per-reference indirection. Measured:
  one $F$ costs $0.0294$\,s on the 450-row deployment model and
  $0.211$\,s on the 1{,}951-row logistics model. For comparison, the
  hand-coded twin of the deployment game completes entire chain
  \emph{solves} in 18--29\,s (Table~\ref{tab:deploy-runs}).
\item \textbf{No sharing across rows.} The interpreter re-evaluates
  common subexpressions per row. The logistics model's flow
  cost-benefit family contains one 900-term whole-network sum that is
  identical in all 900 of its rows; most of the $0.211$\,s is that one
  sum computed 900 times.
\item \textbf{Black-box differentiation.} The engines receive $F$ as a
  callable, so Jacobians come from finite differences --- fourth-order,
  hence $\sim 4 \cdot \mathrm{dim}$ evaluations per Newton iteration.
  Measured on the deployment model: $\sim$1{,}800 evaluations, 53--68\,s
  per semismooth iteration; a bounded 20-iteration probe took 24 minutes
  and, as on deploy\_v07, no single engine escapes this family's plateaus
  --- the alternating chain would, but a chain run at this cost is hours.
\end{enumerate}

The decisive comparison is the reference system itself. GAMS also
interprets its models --- it never compiles them --- yet PATH solves the
deployment game in seconds, because GAMS supplies PATH with analytic,
\emph{sparse} derivatives by automatic differentiation of the same
expression trees. Interpretation alone is factors one and two; factor
three, the one that multiplies by the dimension, is a choice of
derivative supply, and it is the one that matters most. The recorded
options, deferred with their measurements: automatic differentiation of
the \code{Expr} trees (exact sparse Jacobians, removing factor three), a
flattened-tape evaluator with common-subexpression sharing (factors one
and two), or both.

The logistics model demonstrates the same point from the other side:
its equations are affine, so the probe assembled the exact constant $M$
column by column ($\mathrm{dim}+1$ evaluations, once, 489\,s), verified
affinity at a random point (relative deviation $2.6\times 10^{-15}$), and
handed $(M, q)$ to the interior-point engine --- which converged in 16
iterations and $5.1$\,s. Where structure replaces the black box, the
interpreted representation costs a one-time assembly and nothing more.

Two smaller lessons from the same runs. First, the probe itself had to be
redesigned once: its first version ran the automatic engine dispatcher,
whose chain fallback made the intended iteration bound unbounded, and it
printed nothing between iterations --- both models appeared hung. The
second version calls engines directly and prints a heartbeat with elapsed
time every iteration; the lesson is the observability lesson of
Section~\ref{sec:t-lessons} applied to measurement tools. Second, the
interior-point run at the tightest tolerance walked past its achievable
floor ($\sim 3\times 10^{-10}$ squared on this scaling) into a non-finite
corrector solve; one step looser converged cleanly --- the same end-game
behavior recorded for the engine in Chapter~\ref{ch:manual}.

The decision of record (2026-07-09): production problems arrive as
hand-built C++ structures reading data files --- the fleet pipeline
pattern, with the translation recipe of Section~\ref{sec:gams-recipe} ---
and the front end serves as the reference, prototyping, and cross-check
path, where a solve measured in minutes is acceptable and four of the
five corpus models already reproduce their GAMS references.

\section{Cross-cutting lessons}
\label{sec:t-lessons}

Ten lessons recur across the case studies; each is stated with its
evidence.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A change of engine class succeeds where tuning does not.}
  No amount of projection-contraction tuning touched the banded stall; the
  central path is insensitive to the geometry that causes it
  (Table~\ref{tab:banded}). Symmetrically, no semismooth line-search
  variant escaped the deployment stationary points that composition
  avoided (Table~\ref{tab:deploy-runs}, run 2).
\item \textbf{Exploiting structure is superior to screening.} The screen
  approximates the problem to fit the solver; the structured factory made
  the solver fit the problem --- faster ($394\times$ single-commodity,
  four orders of magnitude for the fleet) and exact
  (Sections~\ref{sec:t-flow} and~\ref{sec:t-fleet}).
\item \textbf{Off the monotone class, composition outperforms any single
  engine.} The chain's value is precisely that its stages' failure sets do
  not intersect at handoff points (Chapter~\ref{ch:math}); no component
  becomes a better standalone solver by being in it.
\item \textbf{Stalls are not errors.} A collapsed step length or failed
  line search at a real iterate is information; throwing on it cost a
  converging run (run 4) and costs every wrapper a catch block. The
  library-wide contract --- bad values throw, stalls return --- derives
  directly from this chapter's experience.
\item \textbf{Return the best point visited.} Nonmonotone strategies may
  end above their best (run 2's $\lambda$-restart: returned
  $1.7\times 10^{4}$, had visited $8.2\times 10^{3}$); both the semismooth
  solver and the chain now report the best, judged by one fixed
  functional.
\item \textbf{Determinism makes verbatim retries unproductive.} Runs
  6--7's digit-identical outputs proved the round map was at a fixed
  point; scaled, fixed-seed perturbation breaks the fixed point without
  sacrificing reproducibility or convergence.
\item \textbf{Observability is a solver feature.} Flushed heartbeats (the
  buffered-silence incident), named residual breakdowns (which found the
  poles), and correctly reported \code{converged} flags turned each failed
  run into the next run's specification. None of it costs anything when
  disabled.
\item \textbf{Check against verified answers, structured for
  multiplicity.} Fixed answers where the answer is unique (both
  case-study checks), fixed \emph{counts} of passing rows where engines
  legitimately differ, self-extending rosters where equilibria multiply
  (Section~\ref{sec:t-infra}).
\item \textbf{Process: verify algebra before code; inject failure to test
  protocols.} The flow factory's Maxima script \cite{maxima} found sign
  conventions before any C++ ran; the fake-component method
  (Section~\ref{sec:t-protocol}) is how every guard in the library is
  actually exercised.
\item \textbf{Match the derivative supply to the engine's demand.} An
  interpreted model representation multiplies three costs: tree-walking
  evaluation, re-evaluation of subexpressions shared across rows, and ---
  dominating, because it scales with the dimension --- finite-difference
  Jacobians at $\sim 4 \cdot \mathrm{dim}$ evaluations per iteration.
  The reference system interprets its models too and still solves in
  seconds, because it supplies analytic sparse derivatives; where the
  model is affine, extracting the constant matrix once removed the
  black box entirely (Section~\ref{sec:t-gms-cost}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Known limitations and open items}
\label{sec:t-open}

Recorded with their planned dispositions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The IPM cannot warm-start} (structural: it needs a strictly
  interior, near-path start). Re-solve loops that would benefit from warm
  starts use the projection engines or pay the full interior-point cost.
\item \textbf{Dense $M$ at scale: resolved for the interior-point engine,
  open elsewhere.} \code{mehrotraIpm} accepts $M$ as an apply operator
  (Section~\ref{sec:t-fleet}; the dense overload is unchanged bit for
  bit), and the fleet pipeline runs on it by default. The projection
  engines and the single-commodity flow pipeline still hold $M$ densely;
  extending them is mechanical when a problem requires it.
\item \textbf{\code{dHan06}'s refactor-per-iteration cost} ($\sim$12$\times$
  \code{bsHe94b} in wall time at equal iterations on the SAOE runs) is
  accepted; cache/reuse across $\beta$ updates is a recorded option if the
  engine is ever needed at scale.
\item \textbf{Comparative benchmark rows} (chain / \code{bshe94b} /
  \code{ssn} bounded probes on the 200-banded instance) are planned as
  report data; no decision depends on them.
\item \textbf{The acceleration wrappers} (Anderson/Halpern) from the engine
  roadmap are designed, literature-verified, and unbuilt --- the roadmap's
  one remaining item.
\item \textbf{The deployment game through the GMS front end} is blocked
  on evaluation performance (Section~\ref{sec:t-gms-cost}); the recorded
  options are automatic differentiation of the expression trees (exact
  sparse Jacobians) and a flattened-tape evaluator with subexpression
  sharing, with the measured costs (one $F$: $0.0294$\,s; one Jacobian:
  53--68\,s) as the baseline any such work is judged against. The
  hand-built twin remains the production path.
\item \textbf{More PPD versions are coming}; each arrives as data plus a
  \code{VIModel} builder and a new roster, per the recipe of
  Chapter~\ref{ch:manual}.
\end{itemize}
