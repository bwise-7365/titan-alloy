# Action and Inaction

### Why technical mastery did not defeat Iran, and what could compel it instead

In June 2025, American B-2s placed fourteen massive penetrators into Fordow and Natanz, with Tomahawks against Isfahan, in about twenty-five minutes. In February 2026, the opening salvo of a second campaign killed the Supreme Leader and much of the senior command in a single night. By any technical standard these were near-perfect operations. By the standard that actually matters they failed, because a year later Iran had not been defeated, its program had not been ended, and its government had not fallen. This article concerns the distance between those two verdicts. The distance is not a gap in capability, which is not in question. It is a gap between two kinds of power, the power to act and the power to withhold, and between the two lies the one thing that force cannot reach, namely the adversary's will. We argue the point in three steps: first, that action has a ceiling; second, that the ceiling exists because the decision in war is made in the will, where force cannot operate; and third, that the power which does reach the will is inaction, applied through dependency.

## Action and its ceiling

We begin by conceding the strength, because the strength is real and the argument is stronger for granting it. The United States can find and destroy very nearly anything it can locate. The two Iran campaigns were neither flukes nor overreaches. They were the system performing as designed, delivering precise effects against hardened and dispersed targets on schedule. Nothing said here disputes that mastery. The question we press is narrower and harder, namely what that mastery actually buys at the level of national outcomes. Granting the capability in full is the honest place to begin, because it sharpens the puzzle rather than softening it.

The trouble begins not with the munitions but with how we measure them. Battle-damage assessment (how much was destroyed, how many were killed, which nodes were cut) is the right yardstick for acquiring a weapon. It tells us whether a system performs, it lets us compare candidates, and it disciplines a procurement decision with numbers. The error is to carry that same yardstick into strategy, where the relevant question is not how much was destroyed but whether the destruction changed anyone's choices. These two questions are by no means the same, yet the institutional pull fuses them, and for a reason that is defensive rather than analytical. To abandon the destruction metric is to concede that the measure on which programs and prior decisions were built was the wrong tool for the present job, so the metric is protected instead of revisited. The pattern was on display after the first campaign, when an early Defense Intelligence Agency assessment held that the strikes had set the program back only months, that Fordow was damaged but functional, and that a large quantity of enriched uranium was unaccounted for. The response was not to reconsider the strategic question but to defend the claim that the sites had been "obliterated" and to fault the assessors. A measure built to compare weapons had been handed a war to adjudicate, and it returned the only kind of answer it knows.

Even granting the destruction at face value, it does not climb on its own. Edward Luttwak's framework in *Strategy: The Logic of War and Peace* distinguishes levels (technical, tactical, operational, theater, and grand-strategic), and its central and uncomfortable claim is that success at a lower level does not ascend to a higher one automatically. A perfect strike is a technical fact. Whether it yields an operational advantage, and whether that advantage in turn yields a strategic result, are separate matters that the strike itself does not settle. By the paradoxical logic Luttwak describes, brilliance below can even invite reversal above. The clean decapitation of Iran's leadership is the case in point, since removing the men at the top did not collapse the system but cleared the way for a harder-line succession. Hence the better the strike, the worse the strategic position it produced, which is to say that technical excellence purchased strategic loss.

None of this is novel theory, and it has been demonstrated on the ground twice within living memory. The French in Indochina and the Americans in Vietnam (in 1954 and again in 1975) each held overwhelming technical and material superiority, and each lost. What those wars teach is not that firepower is useless but that firepower is not what decides, since an adversary who will not be converted by destruction is not converted by more of it. So if superior force does not by itself decide a war, then something else does, and locating that something is the next step.

## The will, where the decision lives

That something is the will, and the simplest way to see it is to ask precisely when a war ends. A war ends in one of two ways: when the weaker side chooses to accept defeat, or when the weaker side is physically eliminated to the last person. The second is genocide, a crime so few states will commit that we can set it aside as a real instrument of policy. What remains is the first, and it carries a striking implication. If a war ends only when the loser agrees to be the loser, then the decision as to who has lost belongs, in the last analysis, to the weaker party. We may call this the loser's veto. Iran held it throughout, and across two campaigns, the death of its Supreme Leader, the loss of much of its command, and tens of thousands of its own dead, it never offered the one thing that ending the war on American terms required, namely its own concession. The decision was always Tehran's to give, and Tehran withheld it.

The veto is not exercised by clever maneuver but by simply continuing, which is why endurance defeats action against a determined opponent. Each strike adds to the tally of destruction, but the tally is not the war, and the war turns on a choice that more destruction does not compel. Bomb an enduring adversary and we produce more rubble, more grievance, and more of the very resolve we meant to break, while the strategic ledger does not move. The endgame made the asymmetry plain, because by mid-2026, after everything the campaigns had destroyed, it was Washington that signed a memorandum offering sanctions relief and a commitment to Iranian reconstruction. The party that merely persisted ended by negotiating the terms of its own survival, which is what the loser's veto looks like once it is cashed in.

Beneath endurance lies the reason it works, and that reason is the hinge of the whole argument: force acts on the body, not on the will. Munitions destroy infrastructure, equipment, and people, all of which are physical. The decision to quit or to persist is not physical. It is an act of will, and no weapon in any inventory is aimed at it. This is why a campaign can be flawless in every measurable respect and still fail, since it operates entirely in the register of the body while the outcome is settled in the register of the will. Put this way, the hinge swings in both directions. It closes the case against action, because action cannot reach the place where wars are decided, and it opens the case for the opposite, because if the will moves only when what it needs changes, then the lever that matters is not the one that destroys what the adversary has but the one that governs what the adversary needs.

## Inaction, and the network it runs through

That lever is the power of inaction. Where action threatens by striking, inaction threatens by stopping, so that if we supply something the other side cannot do without, then our withholding of it, and not our attacking of it, becomes the operative threat. The threat works precisely because we need never lift a hand. The engineered form of this arrangement is dependency, and to make an adversary depend on us is to convert our own inaction into leverage over his will, which is the register that force could not enter. Dependency, in short, is the strategy of inaction, and it takes economic and military forms that share a single logic.

The economic form runs from mild to strong. In its mild version it is the oldest argument for commerce, that nations which trade do not lightly forfeit the gains from trade and from relative advantage, so that trade gives them a calculable interest in peace. Albert O. Hirschman traced this case in *The Passions and the Interests*, showing that long before modern economics, markets were defended as a way to bind the destructive passions of rulers with sober interest. The effect is real but weak (wars have erupted across the centuries among trading partners), so mild dependency binds only at the margin and will not hold a determined adversary. The strong version differs in kind, not in degree. It is hollowing out, the destruction of a population's capacity to provide for itself, after which the population is simply dependent. It has at least three modes. In the British mode, advanced machinery made goods so cheap that local producers, even of food and cloth, could not compete and were driven from their own markets. In the Chinese mode, the same hollowing was achieved not by superior machines but by extraordinarily cheap labor. In the Saudi mode it rested on neither, but on a monopoly of one precious commodity (i.e. oil), so that almost all income arrives either through a small elite in the commodity sector or through the spending of underpaid foreign labor, and where income descends from above, the most rewarding activity for most people becomes the courting of those who dispense it. The three modes differ in mechanism and converge in effect, since human capital atrophies, personal dependency deepens, and control follows from the dependency, which was the point throughout.

The military form runs in parallel and resolves to the same move. In the Athenian mode, the dominant power monetizes the security obligations of others. Athens hired out its forces for pay, and over time its allies, preferring to avoid the burden of service, paid money in place of furnishing ships. Thucydides records the consequence at 1.99, that Athens grew its strength on the allies' funds while the allies, no longer practiced in war, found themselves without resources or experience whenever they tried to break away. Athens accumulated both skill and wealth as its partners lost both (Athens holding the relative advantage at sea, Sparta on land), until the partners could no longer act on their own account. The same phrase, relative advantage, that governed the economic case governs this one, and the repetition is not accidental, since dependency is the deliberate manufacture of a relative advantage that compounds over time. In the British mode the sequence is colder still. We use superior force to install an unpopular regime, then we calibrate support so that the regime feels permanently insecure before a hostile population, yet never quite insecure enough to fall. Such a regime cannot stand on its own and must lean on its patron for survival against its own people, so the patron rules not by firing a shot but by adjusting a dial. Across economy and arms the maneuver is one and the same: make the other need us, then govern through the threat of withdrawal.

Applied to Iran, the case grows clear, but only if we describe its shape correctly. It is tempting to say that the Iran situation "inverts" some relationship, as though a single arrow had reversed direction. That misstates the structure, which is a directed network of threatened inaction, with several arrows live at once and, where both parties need the exchange, arrows running both ways in cycles. The canonical arrow in such a network is the one A. J. P. Taylor identified in *The Origins of the Second World War*, where a party voids an agreement not by defiance but by non-performance, by simply declining to do what was promised, and so leaves the other side to choose between acquiescence and a war to enforce a peace that its own public judges futile. Iran sits at both ends of such arrows at the same time. Toward Washington it occupies Germany's chair, since it can nullify any deal it signs by inaction, by declining to honor enrichment limits, by reclosing the Strait of Hormuz, by rearming its proxies, and it demonstrated exactly this within days of the 2026 memorandum when it again declared the Strait closed. Toward Beijing it occupies France's chair. The Chinese surveillance apparatus on which the Iranian state relied to identify and suppress its own citizens is the one pillar the regime cannot do without, and that pillar sits under another government's control, so that withdrawing the support would leave the regime precarious. As with Germany facing France, the holder of that arrow need not act in order to coerce. It need only let it be known that it might stop. The campaigns reached the program, the hardware, and the leadership, which is to say everything physical, and they touched neither of the two pillars (internal coercion and external dependency) on which the outcome actually turned.

## Two lines of effort

Pull the three steps together and the prescription follows, though it is not the prescription that either camp usually wants. Action has a ceiling because it cannot reach the will, while inaction can reach the will because it works on what the adversary needs, so the answer is not to choose between them but to wield both, deliberately and together. Two lines of effort follow, and they belong to no single domain, applying on land, at sea, in the air, and in the economy alike.

The first line is to keep doing what the system already does well, though with fewer illusions about what it secures: to seek out tactical opportunities to act, and where the adversary presents no opening, to manufacture one. Army doctrine is explicit that when no assailable flank exists, the attacking force creates one, and generalized, this is Liddell Hart's indirect approach, which dislocates an opponent by surprise and along the line he least expects rather than battering his strength head on. This line of effort is necessary and it is not going away. What the Iran campaigns show is only that it is by no means sufficient on its own, and that measuring it by what it destroys will keep mistaking activity for result.

The second line is the one the destruction paradigm cannot see, namely to seek out opportunities to leverage strategic inaction, including the patient construction of economic and military dependencies. The aim is to arrange the adversary's situation so that continued defiance, and not merely the prospect of being bombed, becomes the cost he cannot bear, until compliance is the move he himself prefers and the binding threat against him is our withheld cooperation rather than our ordnance. The whole argument reduces to a simple proposition. A weaker party that holds the loser's veto cannot be destroyed into surrender, because surrender is a decision and decisions are not physical, and it can be brought to choose compliance only when refusing to comply is what it can no longer afford. Those costs are not delivered by aircraft. They are built, over time, into the structure of the adversary's needs, and a strategy that knows the difference between acting and withholding is the one that knows which of its instruments can reach the place where the war is actually decided.
