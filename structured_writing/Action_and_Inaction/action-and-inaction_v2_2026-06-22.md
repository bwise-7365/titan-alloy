# Action and Inaction

### Why technical mastery did not defeat Iran, and what could compel it instead

In June 2025, American B-2s put fourteen massive penetrators into Fordow and Natanz and Tomahawks into Isfahan in about twenty-five minutes. In February 2026, the opening salvo of a second campaign killed the Supreme Leader and much of the senior command inside a single night. By any technical standard these were near-perfect operations. By the standard that matters, they failed: a year on, Iran had not been defeated, its program had not been ended, and its government had not fallen. The gap between those two verdicts is the subject of this article. It is not a gap in capability. It is a gap between two kinds of power — the power to act and the power to withhold — and between the two lies the one thing force cannot reach, which is the adversary's will. The argument proceeds in three moves: first, that action has a ceiling; second, that the ceiling exists because decision lives in the will; and third, that the power which does reach the will is inaction, exercised through dependency.

## Action and its ceiling

Begin by conceding the strength, because the strength is real. The United States can find and destroy very nearly anything it can locate. The two Iran campaigns were not flukes or overreaches; they were the system working as designed, delivering precise effects against hardened and dispersed targets on a schedule. Whatever else is said here, none of it disputes that mastery. The question this article presses is narrower and harder: what does that mastery actually buy at the level of national outcomes? Granting the capability in full is the honest place to start, because it sharpens rather than softens the puzzle.

The trouble begins not with the bombs but with how we measure them. Battle-damage assessment — how much was destroyed, how many were killed, which nodes were severed — is the right yardstick for *acquiring* a weapon. It tells you whether a system performs, lets you compare candidates, and disciplines a procurement decision with numbers. The error is to carry that same yardstick into *strategy*, where the relevant question is not how much was destroyed but whether the destruction changed anyone's choices. The two questions are distinct, yet the institutional pull fuses them, and for a reason that is more defensive than analytical: to abandon the destruction metric is to concede that the metric on which careers, programs, and prior decisions were built was the wrong tool for the job. So the metric is protected rather than revisited. The pattern was visible after the first campaign, when an early Defense Intelligence Agency assessment held that the strikes had set the program back only months, left Fordow damaged but functional, and left a large quantity of enriched uranium unaccounted for — and the response was to defend the claim that the sites had been "obliterated" and to attack the assessors. A measure built to compare weapons had been handed a war to adjudicate, and it returned the only kind of answer it knows.

Even if the destruction is taken at face value, however, it does not climb on its own. Edward Luttwak's framework in *Strategy: The Logic of War and Peace* distinguishes levels — technical, tactical, operational, theater, and grand-strategic — and the central, uncomfortable point is that success at a lower level does not ascend to a higher one automatically. A perfect strike is a technical fact; whether it produces an operational advantage, and whether that advantage produces a strategic result, are separate contingencies that the strike itself does not settle. Worse, by the paradoxical logic Luttwak describes, brilliance below can invite reversal above. The clean decapitation of Iran's leadership is the case in point: removing the men at the top did not collapse the system but cleared the way for a harder-line succession, so the better the strike, the worse the strategic position it produced. Technical excellence had purchased strategic loss.

None of this is novel theory; it has been demonstrated on the ground, twice, in living memory. The French in Indochina and the Americans in Vietnam each held overwhelming technical and material superiority, and each lost. The lesson those wars left is not that firepower is useless but that firepower is not what decides; an adversary who will not be converted by destruction is not converted by more of it. So the section closes on the question it has forced into the open. If superior force does not, by itself, decide the outcome of a war, then something else does — and locating that something is the next move.

## The will, where the decision lives

That something is the will, and the cleanest way to see it is to ask precisely when a war ends. It ends in one of two ways: when the weaker side chooses to accept defeat, or when the weaker side is physically eliminated down to the last person. The second is genocide, a crime so few states will commit that it can be set aside as a real instrument of policy. What remains is the first, and it has a startling implication: if the war ends only when the loser agrees to be the loser, then the decision as to who has lost belongs, in the end, to the weaker party. Call it the loser's veto. Iran held it throughout. Across two campaigns, the death of its Supreme Leader, the loss of much of its command, and tens of thousands of its own dead, it never tendered the one thing required to end the war on American terms — its own concession. The decision was always Tehran's to give, and Tehran withheld it.

The veto is not exercised by clever maneuver; it is exercised by simply continuing. This is why endurance defeats action against a determined opponent. Each strike adds to the tally of destruction, but the tally is not the war; the war turns on a choice that more destruction does not compel. Bomb an enduring adversary and you produce more rubble, more grievance, and more of the very resolve you meant to break, while the strategic ledger does not move. The endgame made the asymmetry plain. By mid-2026, after everything the campaigns had destroyed, it was Washington that signed a memorandum offering sanctions relief and a commitment to Iranian reconstruction. The party that merely persisted ended up negotiating the terms of its own survival, which is what the loser's veto looks like when it is cashed in.

Underneath endurance is the reason it works, and the reason is the hinge of this whole argument: force acts on the body, not on the will. Munitions destroy infrastructure, equipment, and people — all of it physical, all of it body. The decision to quit or to persist is not physical; it is an act of will, and no weapon in any inventory is aimed at it. This is why a campaign can be flawless in every measurable respect and still fail: it operates entirely in the register of the body while the outcome is settled in the register of the will. Stated this way, the hinge swings both directions. It closes the case against action — action cannot reach the place where wars are decided — and it opens the case for its opposite, because if the will moves only when what it needs changes, then the lever that matters is not the one that destroys what the adversary has but the one that controls what the adversary needs.

## Inaction, and the network it runs through

That lever is the power of inaction. Where action threatens by striking, inaction threatens by stopping: if you supply something the other side cannot do without, then your *withholding* it — not your attacking it — becomes the operative threat, and the threat works precisely because you never have to lift a hand. The engineered form of this arrangement is dependency. To make an adversary depend on you is to convert your own inaction into leverage over his will, which is exactly the register that force could not enter. Dependency, in other words, is the strategy of inaction, and it comes in economic and military forms that share a single logic.

The economic form runs from mild to strong. In its mild version it is the oldest argument for commerce: nations that trade do not lightly forfeit the gains from trade and from relative advantage, and so trade gives them a calculable interest in peace. Albert O. Hirschman traced this case in *The Passions and the Interests*, showing that long before modern economics, markets were defended as a way to bind rulers' destructive passions with sober interest. The effect is real but weak — wars have erupted across the centuries among trading partners — so mild dependency binds only at the margin and cannot hold a determined adversary. The strong version is different in kind, not degree. It is hollowing out: the destruction of a population's capacity to provide for itself, after which the population is simply dependent. It has at least three modes. In the British mode, advanced machinery made goods so cheap that local producers, even of food and cloth, could not compete and were driven from their own markets. In the Chinese mode, the same hollowing was achieved not by superior machines but by extraordinarily cheap labor. In the Saudi mode it rested on neither, but on a monopoly of one precious commodity, so that almost all income arrives either through a small elite in the commodity sector or through the spending of underpaid foreign labor — and where income descends from above, the most rewarding activity for most people becomes the courting of those who dispense it. The three modes differ in mechanism and converge in effect: human capital atrophies, personal dependency deepens, and control follows from dependency, which was the point all along.

The military form parallels the economic one and resolves to the same move. In the Athenian mode, the dominant power monetizes the security obligations of others. Athens hired out its forces for pay, and over time its allies, preferring to avoid the burden of service, paid money in place of furnishing ships; Thucydides records the consequence at 1.99, that Athens grew its strength on the allies' funds while the allies, no longer practiced in war, found themselves without resources or experience when they tried to break away. Athens accumulated both skill and wealth as its partners lost both — Athens holding the relative advantage at sea, Sparta on land — until the partners could no longer act on their own account. The same phrase, relative advantage, that governs the economic case governs this one, and it is no accident: dependency is the deliberate manufacture of a relative advantage that compounds. In the British mode the sequence is colder still. Use superior force to install an unpopular regime, then calibrate support so that the regime feels permanently insecure before a hostile population, yet never quite insecure enough to fall. Such a regime cannot stand on its own and must lean on its patron for survival against its own people; the patron then rules not by firing a shot but by adjusting a dial. Across economy and arms, the maneuver is identical — make the other need you, then govern through the threat of withdrawal.

Applied to Iran, the case clarifies, but only if its shape is described correctly. It is tempting to say that the Iran situation "inverts" some relationship, as if a single arrow had reversed. That misstates the structure, which is a directed network of threatened inaction, with several arrows live at once and, where both parties need the exchange, arrows running both ways in cycles. The canonical arrow in such a network is the one A. J. P. Taylor identified in *The Origins of the Second World War*: a party can void an agreement not by defiance but by non-performance, by simply declining to do what was promised, leaving the other side to choose between acquiescence and a war to enforce a peace that its own public judges futile. Iran sits at both ends of such arrows simultaneously. Toward Washington it occupies Germany's chair — it can nullify any deal it signs by inaction, declining to honor enrichment limits, reclosing the Strait of Hormuz, rearming its proxies — and it demonstrated exactly this within days of the 2026 memorandum by again declaring the Strait closed. Toward Beijing it occupies France's chair. The Chinese surveillance apparatus that the Iranian state relied on to identify and suppress its own citizens is the one pillar the regime cannot do without, and it sits under another government's control; strip away that support and the regime is precarious. As with Germany facing France, the holder of that arrow need not act to coerce. It need only let it be known that it might stop. The campaigns reached the program, the hardware, and the leadership — everything physical — and touched neither of the two pillars, internal coercion and external dependency, on which the outcome actually turned.

## Two lines of effort

Pull the three moves together and the prescription writes itself, though it is not the prescription either camp usually wants. Action has a ceiling because it cannot reach the will; inaction can reach the will because it works on what the adversary needs; therefore the answer is not to choose between them but to wield both, deliberately and at once. Two lines of effort follow, and they belong to no single domain — land, sea, air, and economy alike.

The first is to keep doing what the system already does well, and to do it with fewer illusions: search out tactical opportunities to act, and where the adversary presents no opening, manufacture one. Army doctrine is explicit that when no assailable flank exists, the attacking force creates one; generalized, this is Liddell Hart's indirect approach, which dislocates an opponent by surprise and along the line he least expects rather than battering his strength head-on. This line of effort is necessary and it is not going away. What the Iran campaigns show is only that it is not, by itself, sufficient, and that measuring it by what it destroys will keep mistaking activity for result.

The second is the line the destruction paradigm cannot see: search out opportunities to leverage strategic inaction, including the patient construction of economic and military dependencies. The aim is to arrange the adversary's situation so that *continued defiance* — not merely the prospect of being bombed — becomes the cost he cannot bear, until compliance is the move he prefers and the binding threat against him is your withheld cooperation rather than your ordnance. Here is the whole argument in a sentence: a weaker party that holds the loser's veto cannot be destroyed into surrender, because surrender is a decision and decisions are not physical; it can be brought to choose compliance only when refusing to comply is what it can no longer afford. Those costs are not delivered by aircraft. They are built, over time, into the structure of the adversary's needs — and a strategy that knows the difference between acting and withholding is the one that knows which of its tools can actually reach the place where the war is decided.
